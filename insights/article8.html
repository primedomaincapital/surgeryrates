The Knee Replacement That Costs $2,321 in Colorado and $37,644 in Arizona

Same operation, same billing code, same implant class. The typical posted cash price in Arizona runs sixteen times the typical price in Colorado — and the usual explanations do not survive the data.

Two hospitals, two states, one billing code. In Colorado, the typical posted cash price for a total knee replacement is $2,321. In Arizona, it is $37,644. The implant comes from the same manufacturers. The operation takes the same two hours. The difference is not the knee.

Two hospitals, two states, one billing code. In Colorado, the typical posted cash price for a total knee replacement is $2,321. In Arizona, it is $37,644. The implant comes from the same manufacturers. The operation takes the same two hours. The surgeon uses the same code, 27447, and the hospital bills the same facility charge category. The difference is not the knee. It is everything around the knee — and the data says the usual explanations for the gap do not survive contact with the numbers.

Total knee arthroplasty is the most common major operation in American orthopedics, and the most studied price in American healthcare. More than a million knee replacements are performed in the United States each year, and the price has been measured so many ways that the measurements themselves disagree. A 2023 international analysis put the average American total knee replacement at $19,568. A separate cost analysis of knee replacements without complications in American hospitals found an average of $68,016, with a range from $39,927 to $195,264. Both figures are real. Neither describes a state. The first is a national average from one study; the second is a hospital-cost average from another, measuring a different basket of services. Quoting them as two states' prices would be precise about the numbers and wrong about what they mean.

The state comparison needs a single dataset, a single measure, and a single vintage. The price-transparency files hospitals have published since 2021 provide it. An aggregator that has read the files of 972 hospitals reports, for CPT 27447, a national typical cash price of $12,866, a typical commercial insurer rate of $17,010, and a typical list price of $21,433. The same dataset, broken by state, is where the geography appears: Colorado $2,321, Maine $2,327, Iowa $2,286, Minnesota $3,290, Michigan $5,628 at the low end; Indiana $34,284 and Arizona $37,644 at the high end. The spread between the cheapest and most expensive typical state price is more than sixteenfold.

Testing the usual explanations

The first explanation offered for the gap is labor cost. It fails quickly. Colorado, the cheapest state on the cash-price table, is not a low-wage state; its hospitals pay nurses and technicians at rates close to the national average, and Denver is one of the more expensive hospital labor markets in the Mountain West. Arizona, the most expensive, is not a high-wage outlier either. If wages set the price, the map would look like a wage map. It does not. The correlation between state hospital wages and state knee prices is weak, and the extremes — Colorado cheap, Arizona expensive — run the wrong way.

The second explanation is the facility fee: hospitals charge more than ambulatory surgery centers, and states with more hospital-based surgery charge more. There is truth in the mechanism — outpatient centers run 30 to 50 percent cheaper than hospitals for the same procedure, and the facility fee is the largest single line on the bill — but it does not explain the state gap either. Colorado's low prices are hospital prices, from hospital files, not surgery-center prices. Arizona's high prices are hospital prices too. The facility mix differs by state, but not by sixteenfold.

The third explanation is the implant. Knee implants range from $1,800 to $12,000 depending on type, and the choice of implant — standard versus robotic-assisted, cemented versus cementless — moves the bill by thousands. But implants are sold nationally by the same manufacturers, Zimmer Biomet, Stryker, and DePuy Synthes, at prices that vary far less by state than the surgery price does. An implant cannot explain a $35,000 gap between two states when the most expensive implant on the market costs $12,000.

What actually moves the number

What moves the number is market power and payer mix, and the data points at both. The states with the lowest typical cash prices — Colorado, Maine, Iowa, Minnesota — are states where cash-pay surgery centers and transparent pricing have taken hold, and where hospitals face competition from ambulatory centers that post their prices. The states with the highest — Arizona, Indiana, Ohio — are states where hospital systems are more consolidated and where the posted cash price is set with the knowledge that few patients will ever pay it. The cash price is, in effect, a negotiating position: a hospital that expects most patients to arrive through an insurer sets the cash price high, because the cash price is the anchor for the negotiation, not the price of the operation.

The insurer data confirms it. In Arizona, the typical cash price is $37,644 but the typical commercial insurer rate is $13,398 — the insurer pays about a third of the posted cash price. In Maine, the typical cash price is $2,327 and the typical insurer rate is $54,027 — the insurer pays more than twenty times the cash price, because the cash price in Maine is set for a market where cash-pay centers compete on price and the insurer rate is set by a different negotiation entirely. The two numbers are not two measures of the same price. They are two different prices, set by two different processes, for two different buyers. The patient who pays cash and the patient whose insurer pays are buying the same operation in different markets.

The Medicare data tells a third version of the story. Median billed charges from CMS Medicare data put California at $92,239, Arizona at $86,709, Alaska at $77,396, and Alabama at $53,212. Billed charges are not prices — they are the chargemaster's opening ask, and Medicare pays a fraction of them. But the ranking is the same as the cash-price ranking in one respect: the coasts and the consolidated markets bill more, and the difference between the top and the bottom is nearly twofold even before any negotiation begins.

The metro view

Zoom in from states to cities and the spread widens further. A November 2026 scan of federally mandated price files across 25 major metros, covering 379 hospitals, put the national median cash price for a total knee replacement at $19,035 — and the range from $335 in Baltimore to $35,994 in New York. The Baltimore figure is an outlier driven by Maryland's rate-setting system, which caps what hospitals may charge; the New York figure reflects the most expensive hospital market in the country. The metro spread is more than a hundredfold, and it exists within a single dataset, a single month, and a single procedure code.

That is the honest version of the headline. Not $19,000 in one state and $68,000 in another, but $2,321 in Colorado and $37,644 in Arizona on the same measure — and $335 in Baltimore against $35,994 in New York on the same scan. The numbers are smaller than the myth and more damning than the myth, because they are measured the same way, and they still do not make sense as prices for the same operation.

The counterargument: the price is not the product

There is a defense of the gap, and it is not empty. A knee replacement is not a commodity. The surgeon's volume matters — high-volume surgeons have lower complication rates. The hospital's infection rate matters. The rehabilitation protocol matters. A patient who pays $37,644 in Arizona may be buying a different episode of care than a patient who pays $2,321 in Colorado: a different surgeon, a different implant, a different length of stay, a different complication warranty. The price files do not record quality, and a comparison that ignores quality is incomplete.

The defense has limits. Quality varies, but it does not vary sixteenfold between states. The best-measured quality differences in joint replacement — infection rates, readmission rates, revision rates — move outcomes by percentage points, not by multiples. And the cash-price gap exists even within a single city, where the surgeon and the hospital are the same and only the payer differs. The Blue Cross Blue Shield Association's study with Blue Health Intelligence found that an operation averaging about $60,000 in New York City cost about $16,000 in Montgomery, Alabama, and that within more than a dozen cities the price range for one operation exceeded $17,000. The gap is real, it is measured, and quality does not explain it.

What the geography means for the patient

For a patient who can choose where to have the operation, the geography is the single largest lever on the bill — larger than the implant, larger than the facility type, larger than the insurance plan. The practical steps are narrow: get the CPT code, search the procedure on a price-transparency aggregator by hospital, compare the cash price against the insurer's negotiated rate for the patient's own plan, and price the full episode — surgeon, anesthesia, facility, implant, rehabilitation — rather than the facility charge alone. The patient who does that in Colorado pays $2,321 for the hospital's part. The patient who does not, in Arizona, pays $37,644 for the same code. The operation is the same. The homework is the difference.
