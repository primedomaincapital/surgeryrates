The Anesthesia Line Item: Why the Same Operation Bills Differently by Who Puts You Under

The anesthesia bill is priced by the unit, the minute, and the credential of the person holding the mask. The same operation can cost nearly four times as much on this line alone — and the patient never sees the math.

A patient scheduled for a total knee replacement received two anesthesia estimates for the same operation: $307 from Medicare's schedule and more than $1,100 from a commercial quote. The operation had not changed. The clock had not changed. The person holding the mask had.

The anesthesia bill is the only line on a surgical estimate priced by the minute, and it is the line almost nobody reads before the operation. A total knee replacement takes about two hours. Medicare's price for keeping the patient unconscious through those two hours is $307.46. The commercial price for the same two hours, same operation, same billing code, can pass $1,100. The patient did not change. The clock did not change. The person holding the mask did — and the formula that prices the mask is one of the most literal in American medicine.

Anesthesia is billed in units. Every procedure carries a base value set by the American Society of Anesthesiologists, expressed in units that reflect the complexity of the operation and the risk to the patient. Time is then added at one unit per fifteen minutes, measured from the start of anesthesia to the moment the anesthesiologist hands the patient to recovery. The sum is multiplied by a conversion factor — the dollar value of one unit — which Medicare sets annually. That is the whole formula. It is also the whole argument, because every variable in it is a fight.

The conversion factor for 2026 is $20.4976 per unit for most anesthesiologists, under the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services' physician fee schedule final rule released October 31, 2025. Clinicians in qualifying advanced alternative payment models get $20.5998. The increase over 2025's $20.3178 is 0.88 percent — a figure the American Society of Anesthesiologists called meager, noting that Congress's one-year 2.5 percent statutory increase was largely offset by other adjustments in the rule. The factor also varies by locality: $19.53 in Alabama, $21.26 in Los Angeles. A unit is not worth the same everywhere.

Apply the formula to a total knee replacement, anesthesia code 01402, and the arithmetic is exact. The code carries 7 base units. A two-hour operation adds 8 time units. Fifteen units at $20.4976 is $307.46 — the full Medicare allowance when an anesthesiologist personally performs the case. A lumbar spine fusion, code 00670, carries 13 base units; a three-hour case adds 12 time units, for 25 units and $512.44. A colonoscopy, code 00811, carries 4 base units and a half hour of time, for 6 units and $122.99. A cataract, code 00142, is 4 base units plus twenty minutes, 5.3 units, $108.64. Medicare pays 80 percent of the allowance; the patient owes the remaining 20 percent as coinsurance.

Who is in the room

The formula's second variable is who provides the anesthesia, and this is where the same operation starts billing differently. Three models dominate American operating rooms. In the first, an anesthesiologist personally performs the case, billing 100 percent of the allowance. In the second, medical direction, one anesthesiologist supervises up to four concurrent cases, each staffed by a certified registered nurse anesthetist or an anesthesiologist assistant; the directing physician and the nurse each bill 50 percent of the allowance. In the third, the nurse anesthetist works without medical direction, billing the full allowance.

Medical direction is where the arithmetic gets interesting for the practice and opaque for the patient. Directing four simultaneous knee replacements, each billing 15 units, the anesthesiologist collects 50 percent of each — $153.73 per case, $614.92 for the same two hours that would pay $307.46 for one personally performed case. The model is legal, standard, and disclosed in Medicare's rules. It is also the reason two patients in adjacent operating rooms, both under for two hours, can generate anesthesia bills that differ by a factor of two without any difference in the care they received.

The commercial market prices the same minutes very differently. A Lewin Group analysis of commercial claims, published in 2019 data, found nurse anesthetists paid an average of $58.62 per unit by commercial insurers, against $77.01 per unit for anesthesiologists — a 24 percent gap for the same unit of the same service. Apply those rates to the 15-unit knee replacement and the commercial anesthesia bill runs about $879 when a nurse anesthetist provides the care and about $1,155 when an anesthesiologist does. The Medicare allowance for the same case is $307.46. The spread between the cheapest and most expensive way to put the same patient under for the same operation is nearly fourfold, and it is driven entirely by payer and provider type.

The staffing squeeze

Behind the pricing sits a labor market that has tightened sharply. The Bureau of Labor Statistics put the median nurse anesthetist salary at $181,040 in 2019; by 2024 it had reached $231,700, a 28 percent rise in five years. Recruiters now quote $225 an hour for locum nurse anesthetists. Ambulatory surgery centers, which run on thin margins and cannot pass anesthesia costs to patients the way hospitals can, have responded by paying stipends to anesthesia groups — a payment for the group's availability, on top of the fees billed for each case. VMG Health's surveys found 28 percent of surgery centers expected to pay anesthesia stipends in 2024; by 2025 the share had reached 44 percent.

The stipend is the market's admission that the fee schedule does not cover the cost of the service. A surgery center that pays a stipend is subsidizing anesthesia out of facility revenue, which means the anesthesia cost shows up somewhere else in the patient's bill — or in the center's decision to stop offering the cases that need the most anesthesia coverage. The American Society of Anesthesiologists has argued for years that Medicare's conversion factor undervalues the work; the 2026 rule, with its 0.88 percent increase, did not settle the argument. CMS has already finalized a 1.91 percent cut to the anesthesia conversion factor for 2027, to $20.1056, as the one-year statutory increase expires. The direction of travel is down, and the stipends are the market routing around it.

The surprise on the bill

For patients, the anesthesia line has a second hazard beyond the unit price: whether the anesthesiologist is in the patient's network at all. Anesthesia groups commonly contract separately from the hospital or surgery center, so a patient can choose an in-network facility and still be put under by an out-of-network provider — the classic surprise bill. The No Surprises Act, in force since 2022, prohibits balance billing for out-of-network anesthesia at in-network facilities, which removed the worst version of the problem. But the protection created its own dispute: when the insurer and the provider cannot agree on a price, the case goes to independent dispute resolution, and the volume has overwhelmed the system. The No Surprises Act's arbitration process was built for about 17,000 cases a year. It has handled millions.

The practical consequence is that the anesthesia price is now negotiated after the fact, between the insurer and the provider, in a process the patient never sees — and the outcome of that negotiation shapes the next estimate the patient receives. A patient who asks, before surgery, who will provide the anesthesia and whether that provider is in-network is asking the only question that reliably predicts the line item. The answer is often not in the estimate, because the estimate is written by the facility, and the anesthesia group is a separate business.

The counterargument: supervision is the product

The anesthesiologists' case deserves a hearing, because it is not only about income. Medical direction exists for the cases where one clinician's judgment covers several rooms: the complex patient, the difficult airway, the crisis that needs a physician in the room within minutes. The American Society of Anesthesiologists argues that the model gives patients a physician's oversight at a lower cost than four physicians, and that the 50 percent payment reflects real work — preoperative evaluation, intraoperative supervision, postoperative care — spread across concurrent cases. Nurse anesthetists, through the American Association of Nurse Anesthesiology, answer that they provide the same anesthesia independently in many states, at lower cost, with equivalent safety outcomes, and that the supervision premium is a tax on a model that does not need it.

Both sides can cite data, and the safety literature does not settle it cleanly, because the studies compare different models in different settings with different patient mixes. What the pricing data settles is narrower and more useful: the cost of the same operation varies by hundreds of dollars on this line alone, the variation is driven by who bills and who pays rather than by what happens in the operating room, and the patient is the last person in the chain to learn the price.

What to ask before the operation

Three questions, asked before the surgery date, will tell a patient most of what the estimate will not. First, who will provide the anesthesia — an anesthesiologist, a nurse anesthetist, or a medically directed team — and is that provider in-network for the patient's plan? Second, what is the anesthesia code for the procedure and how many units does it carry, base plus time? Third, is the anesthesia billed separately from the facility fee, and if so, will the group provide its own estimate in writing? The answers will not make the bill small. They will make it predictable, which is the only thing a patient can reliably buy before an operation. The anesthesia line is priced by a formula. The formula is public. The surprise is that nobody shows it to the patient until after the surgery is over.
