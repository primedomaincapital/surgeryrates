How Long the Price Transparency File Takes to Read — and What It Hides

Every hospital in America must publish its prices. The file for one knee replacement can run to hundreds of thousands of rows — and the number that predicts your bill is not the one in the biggest type.

A woman in Ohio downloaded her hospital's price transparency file before a scheduled knee replacement. The file was too large to open in a spreadsheet. She called the billing office instead, and the representative quoted her a price in ninety seconds — a number that appeared nowhere in the file she had just downloaded.

The quote was useful. The gap between the two is the story of hospital price transparency in 2026.

Since January 1, 2021, every licensed hospital in the United States has been required to publish its prices online in two forms. The first is a machine-readable file listing every item and service the hospital charges for, with five prices for each: the gross charge, the discounted cash price, the payer-specific negotiated rate, and the lowest and highest negotiated rates across all payers. The second is a consumer-friendly display of at least 300 shoppable services — the procedures a patient can schedule in advance, like a knee replacement or an MRI — or an online price estimator instead. Both must be free, available without an account, and updated at least once a year. The rule is federal, under 45 CFR Part 180, and it has now been in force for nearly six years.

The files are real, and they are large. A major hospital system's file can run to hundreds of thousands of rows and several gigabytes — too large for a spreadsheet, built for software rather than for people. Each row is one service at one price type, identified by billing code rather than by name. To find a knee replacement, a patient needs the CPT code — 27447 for a total knee arthroplasty — and then needs to know which of the five prices on the row is the one that predicts the bill. Most patients have neither the code nor the decoder. The file answers a question almost nobody knows how to ask.

The five numbers, and which one matters

Every row carries five figures, and they rarely agree. The gross charge is the hospital's list price from its chargemaster — the sticker price, the starting point for every negotiation, and the number almost nobody pays. The discounted cash price is what a patient who pays without insurance is charged, and it is the most useful single number in the file for the uninsured. The payer-specific negotiated charge is the rate the hospital agreed with one named insurance plan, and it is the number that matters for an insured patient — if the patient can find their plan's name in the file. The de-identified minimum and maximum are the lowest and highest negotiated rates across all insurers, without naming them: context, not a price.

Since January 1, 2026, there is a sixth figure to look for. When a negotiated rate is set as a percentage or a formula rather than a dollar amount, the hospital must now publish the dollar amounts actually paid — the median, the 10th percentile, and the 90th percentile — along with the number of payments those figures are based on. CMS began enforcing the change on April 1, 2026. It matters because a large share of hospital prices were previously published as formulas — "140 percent of Medicare," for example — which told a patient nothing about the dollars. The 2026 rule forces the formula to show its work.

The practical reading order is short. Find the CPT code. Find the cash price first, as a reference point. Then find the negotiated rate for the patient's own plan, if it is listed. Ignore the gross charge except as a measure of how far the negotiation traveled. And check the file's date: hospitals must update at least annually, and a file from two years ago is a museum piece.

What the files hide

Compliance is the first problem. PatientRightsAdvocate.org, a nonprofit that has audited hospital postings since 2021, reviewed 2,000 hospitals in its eighth annual report, published September 10, 2026. It found 49.4 percent fully compliant with the rule — the highest rate it has recorded, up from 21.1 percent in November 2024 — which means slightly more than half of the hospitals reviewed were still not fully compliant with a requirement that has been in force since 2021. Only 18 percent posted dollar-and-cents prices for at least half of the items and services in their files. The share of files passing CMS's own validator tool rose from 73.4 percent to 92.8 percent, and 98.9 percent of hospitals presented a compliant shoppable-services display or estimator. The gap is not whether hospitals post something. It is whether what they post is a price.

The second problem is what the file cannot show. The hospital's file lists the facility charge. It does not list the surgeon's fee, the anesthesiologist's bill, or the physical therapy — each billed separately, each with its own price file or none at all. A knee replacement's facility charge is roughly half the total episode cost. The file also cannot know the patient's deductible, coinsurance, or out-of-pocket maximum, which determine what the patient actually pays. The insurer's own price tool, required since January 1, 2024, to cover all covered services with a personalized estimate, answers that question — but only after login, and only for that insurer's members. The hospital file and the insurer tool each hold half the answer, and neither references the other.

The third problem is the file's own errors. Price files contain mistakes — transposed digits, stale rates, placeholder values — and the datasets that aggregate them say so openly. One aggregator notes that the extremes in its data are excluded because price files contain errors. A patient who finds a $30 knee replacement or a $300,000 one in a file has usually found a typo, not a bargain.

A worked example: one knee, three hospitals

Take the total knee replacement, CPT 27447, and read it across three hospitals from the same public dataset — a price-transparency aggregator that has read the files of 972 hospitals. The typical cash price nationally is $12,866. The typical commercial insurer rate is $17,010. The typical list price is $21,433. Those three numbers describe the same operation at the same class of hospital, and they differ by two-thirds from the lowest to the highest — before any negotiation, before any complication, before the surgeon and the anesthesiologist bill separately.

The state spread is wider still. The typical cash price runs $2,321 in Colorado and $37,644 in Arizona. In Ohio, the typical cash price is $23,297, but one hospital in the state posts a cash price of $82,952 for the same code. In Massachusetts, the typical is $11,812 and the highest posted cash price is $60,439. The file does not explain these gaps. It only records them, row by row, in the open, for anyone patient enough to read it. That is both the achievement of the rule and its limit: the data exists, and the explanation does not.

There is a faster way to use the file than reading it. Aggregators — Turquoise Health, the aggregator behind the state table above, and others — have already read the files and republished them as searchable prices by procedure and hospital. A patient who searches for the procedure and the hospital gets the five numbers in seconds, without opening a gigabyte. The file's real audience was never the patient. It was the software that would read the file for the patient.

The counterargument: the file was never the product

The hospitals' defense is worth hearing, because it is partly right. A machine-readable file is infrastructure, not a consumer product; the consumer product is the shoppable-services display or the estimator, which 98.9 percent of hospitals now provide. The file's job is to let third parties build the tools, and those tools now exist. The compliance numbers also cut both ways: PatientRightsAdvocate.org's own headline — half of hospitals noncompliant — rests on its own interpretation of an undefined requirement about algorithms, and the same report shows hospitals exceeding 90 percent compliance on nearly every objective standard CMS actually set, including the validator tool, the file naming, and the transparency link. The gap between the headline and the underlying findings is a measure of how contested the standard itself remains.

Even so, the patient's experience in Ohio is the honest test, and it fails it. A compliant file that cannot be opened, a quote that appears nowhere in the file, and a bill that arrives in five pieces are not a transparency success. They are a transparency system that works for the machines that read it and not yet for the people it was written for.

What to do with the file

Use it in this order. Get the CPT code from the surgeon's office before anything else. Search the procedure on an aggregator rather than downloading the hospital's raw file. Write down the cash price and the negotiated rate for the patient's plan, if listed, and the date of the file. Then call the billing office, ask for a good-faith estimate in writing, and compare the two numbers — the file's and the office's. If they disagree, the written estimate is the one that matters, and the disagreement is worth a second call. The file takes hours to read and minutes to search. The estimate takes ninety seconds to request. Both are free, both are required by law, and the patient who has both is the only person in the transaction who knows what the operation costs before it happens.
